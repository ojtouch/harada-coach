# Phase 1: believe in yourself and assess your self-reliance

## What the method is after

The essence of the Harada Method is **self-reliance**: being competent and reliable enough to make the right decisions on your own, for yourself, for others and for the organization you work in, without close supervision. Self-reliance is not isolation: a self-reliant person knows how to get help and serves others.

The first two steps of the method are:
1. **Believe in yourself.** Believe you can succeed, in your personal as well as your professional life. Harada noticed that his students kept repeating "it's impossible, I'm not cut out for this", often their parents' words. By asking them the question again, insistently ("You really don't want to be a champion?"), they ended up admitting that they dreamed of it.
2. **Become self-reliant.** Put your energy into what you can do yourself, whatever the outside circumstances.

Harada's principle: success is not a miracle, it is a skill acquired through effort. Once you know where and how to put your efforts, all you have to do is repeat.

## The 4 areas of self-reliance

Harada discovered through trial and error that four areas need to be harmonized:

| Area | Content |
|---|---|
| **Mental** (mind) | Focus, confidence, overall attitude |
| **Skills** | Knowledge and know-how, what I can do |
| **Health** (body) | Physical energy, diet, sleep, exercise |
| **Daily life** | Relationships, family, balance, order, what I do outside working hours |

The fourth area is the one that changed everything: the hours spent outside school (or work) weigh on those spent inside. These 4 areas come back in the analysis, the 64 chart and the diary.

## The 6 traits of a self-reliant person

1. **Open to advice and criticism**, even unreasonable criticism (a glass the right way up fills, an upside-down glass lets everything run off). Ego is the enemy.
2. **Responsible for their goals**: a goal set by others becomes their own, never a "quota". When they fail, they look for what they could have done differently, without blaming circumstances, and never see themselves as a victim.
3. **Convinced they can win**, and therefore thoroughly prepared.
4. **A builder of good habits.**
5. **Reflective**: they write, keep a diary, coach themselves.
6. **In harmony with the 4 areas.**

## The method's 10 creeds

1. The way you work shows the way you live.
2. Change yourself before trying to change others.
3. Every tunnel has a light at the end.
4. Clutter crowds the mind, tidiness clears it.
5. Time is money: act now.
6. Your worst enemy is yourself.
7. Always stay positive.
8. Be professional: make a prediction and prepare to make it happen.
9. To succeed, be demanding, gentle and funny.
10. Take time to reflect every day: keep a diary.

Use: suggest the person choose 1 or 2 creeds that speak to them for their phrase of the day or their morning reading routine.

## The 33 qualities self-assessment

### Process

1. Present the list and ask for a score from 1 to 10 for each quality (10 = maximum). To go faster, suggest scoring them in blocks of 5 or 6, with a single answer per block.
2. Identify the **10 lowest scores**.
3. For each one, ask: "What would take you to 10?" and get a concrete action.
4. Keep the answers: they will feed the routines (phase 8) and sometimes the 8 areas.

### Good and bad examples of improvement actions

- Bad: "Realistic (6): be more realistic." Why: it repeats the quality, it is not an action.
- Good: "Realistic (6): before every commitment, check in my calendar that I have the time, otherwise say no." Why: a precise action, triggered by a precise situation.
- Bad: "Prepared (5): prepare myself."
- Good: "Prepared (5): the day before each important meeting, 15 minutes to write down the 3 points I want to get."

## Defining success (if the person has no clear goal)

Three questions from the book:
1. Name a few people you consider highly accomplished.
2. What do they have in common?
3. How do you define success, for yourself?

Then: "Imagine yourself as the best in a field. Which one?"
