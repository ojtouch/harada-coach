# Phases 6 and 7: the 8 areas and the 64 actions (Open Window 64)

## The chart

The Open Window 64 chart (OW64) is a 9×9 grid of cells.
- In the center: the central goal.
- Around it: **8 areas** that support the goal.
- Each area is copied to the center of one of the 8 outer squares, surrounded by **8 actions**.
- Total: 8 × 8 = **64 actions**.

This is the chart that baseball player Shohei Ohtani used in high school.

## Phase 6: choosing the 8 areas

### The book's method
- The areas are **broad** (not specific), to leave room for 8 actions each.
- They are the zones where you need to focus your efforts to develop your skills and experience.
- If an area doesn't inspire 8 actions, it is too narrow: broaden it. If it inspires 30, it may be too broad: split it.
- Remember to cover the 4 areas of self-reliance (mental, skills, health, daily life): a 100% technical chart is a warning sign. The charts in the book almost always contain a "health", "mind" or "family / community" area.

### The coach's brainstorm method
1. First ask the person for their own areas (even 3 or 4).
2. Then propose **2 or 3 sets of 8 areas** built from different angles, for example:
   - **Result levers angle**: what mechanically moves the indicator (for a sales goal: number of clients, average basket, retention…).
   - **Skills angle**: what needs to be learned or mastered.
   - **Whole person angle**: skills + mental + health + daily life + support.
3. Have them choose, merge, rename. The final set is the person's.
4. Check with 3 questions: "If you make strong progress in these 8 areas, is the goal almost guaranteed?", "Is an area missing that could make everything fail?" (reread the obstacles from phase 5), "Does an area duplicate another?"

### Examples of area sets
- Sales goal (sell X in a month): product quality, service, cleanliness of the premises, increasing the number of clients, increasing the average basket, availability, training, management.
- Expertise goal (become the best trainer on a subject): skills, marketing, creating courses, study and research, health and mind, community and family, spirituality or meaning, writing.
- Career goal: skills, family, mentor, strategic planning, ongoing goal setting, transition, leadership, network.

Useful variant (from a company cited in the book): associate each area with **a key indicator** and list repeated activities. Suggest it if the person likes managing by the numbers.

## Phase 7: the 64 actions

### The book's method
- 8 precise actions per area. For some areas you will find more than 8, for others you will struggle.
- Harada recommends filling in the 64 cells in **32 minutes**: the time constraint forces vigorous thinking. Offer this timed challenge to the person, then complete it together.
- Each action must be **directly linked to the goal**. Control question: "Is this really useful? Does it bring me closer to my goal?"
- Two types of actions:
  - **Task**: one-off action with an end date ("prepare the promotion plan by July 1").
  - **Routine**: recurring action, daily or weekly ("call 20 prospects between 9 and 10 a.m.").

### The coach's brainstorm method
Area by area:
1. The person gives their ideas first.
2. The coach completes up to 8 (or more) with concrete suggestions, pointing out that they are options.
3. Rephrase each action as **verb + object + quantity or date**: "Read 2 books on negotiation by May 15" rather than "negotiation".
4. Classify each action: T (task) or R (routine).

### Prioritization by impact (coach's extension, not in the book)

The book prioritizes by start date. The coach adds a rating to find the actions that have the most effect:
- **Impact** on the goal: 1 (low) to 5 (decisive). Question: "If you did only this one, how much would the goal move forward?"
- **Effort**: 1 (5 minutes) to 5 (several weeks).
- **Priority** = high impact and low effort first; then high impact and high effort (to be broken down into subtasks); actions with impact 1 or 2 are kept in the chart but rarely chosen.

Present this rating as an addition to the book. The person has the final say on each rating.

### Good and bad examples of actions

| Bad | Why | Good |
|---|---|---|
| "Marketing" | It's an area, not an action | "Publish 2 client case studies on the website by April 30" (T) |
| "Exercise" | No frequency, no measure | "Walk 30 minutes on Monday, Wednesday and Friday at 7 a.m." (R) |
| "Be more present with the family" | Vague | "Have dinner without phones with the family 5 evenings a week" (R) |
| "Learn" | No object | "Take module 3 of course X and do the exercises by May 12" (T) |

### Collective use of the chart

The chart is also useful in meetings: when ideas remain vague, stop the discussion and have the group fill in a 64 chart. The quality of the proposals rises immediately.
