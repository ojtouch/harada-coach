# Phases 8 and 9: start-up plan, routines, self-talk and support

## 8.1 Start dates

64 actions is more than anyone can launch at once. Go back to the chart and set a **start or end date on as many actions as possible**. This prepares the choice of the first 10.

## 8.2 The 10 tasks to get started

Choose **10 tasks** from the chart to launch right away, listed in chronological order, each with its date ("By {date}: I {action}").

Criteria:
- Priority to high-impact tasks (if the scoring was done) and to those that unlock the others.
- Mix a few quick wins (within 7 days) with structuring tasks.
- Include a task to review the plan halfway through.

These 10 tasks feed the diary: each day, put 1 or 2 of them in your top 5 priority tasks. When a task is done, replace it with a new one drawn from the chart. Redo the list at least once a month.

## 8.3 The 10 routines

### Principle
Most of our behaviors are automatic habits we never question as to whether they serve us. To reach a goal, you have to break destructive habits, and the best way is to install **new positive routines** in their place (replace rather than remove, without picking up a harmful habit instead, like the former smoker who starts snacking).

### Where to look for routines
- Type R actions from the 64 chart.
- Improvement actions from the 33 qualities self-assessment.
- Success behaviors from the analysis (to repeat).
- Countermeasures to failures and obstacles (to install).

### Rules
- **10 routines at most**, ranked by order of importance. Some daily, others once or twice a week.
- Each routine is precise: what, when, how much ("Walk 30 minutes at 7 a.m. on Monday, Wednesday and Friday").
- Mix result-oriented routines with character-oriented routines (diary, gratitude, tidying).
- The book's classic routines: reread your action of the day in the diary, write down in the evening what can be improved tomorrow, read your creed before starting, stretch, congratulate your colleagues, exchange a diary with your children.
- The book's ideal: keep a new routine for **1000 days** so it becomes permanent (see guardrail).
- For each routine, write the **expected effect** ("evaluation column" of the routine check sheet): "After one month, three colleagues do the same thing", "By September 30, my sales have increased by 10%".

### Optional exercises suggested by the book
- List all the routines you repeat in a day, useful or not.
- The "Ohno circle": spend some time observing your work environment without acting, to spot useless movements.

## 9.1 Phrase of the day and self-talk

The book recommends **affirmations** to counter the little voice that says "you're not good enough", and a **phrase of the day** at the top of the diary. Sports teams cheer each other on out loud; you can become your own supporter.

How the coach applies it (with the corresponding guardrail):
- Help the person write 2 or 3 **concrete and plausible** phrases, tied to action: "Today I finish chapter 2 before noon", "One step at a time, I'm moving forward".
- Prepare a reframing phrase for negative thoughts (the book uses "cancel, cancel").
- Avoid flattering, sweeping phrases ("I am invincible").
- The method also encourages you to **congratulate yourself** when no one else does: write down your successes in the diary every evening.

## 9.2 What support do I need?

No one succeeds alone. There are things you cannot do yourself, even if they are essential: instead of giving up, ask for help. People who succeed know how to help and be helped, and prepare in advance the help they will need.

Questions:
- "What are your weaknesses on this project?"
- "What do you need to learn that you don't know yet?"
- "What will you need: information, advice, coaching, proofreading, emotional support, practical help, approval from a manager?"

## 9.3 Who supports me?

- Name **the people** who can help, with the type of help expected from each.
- Choose **a coach**: someone trustworthy, who wants the person to succeed and dares to be honest. Ideally more experienced (a manager, a mentor), but a peer can work.
- The coach rereads the forms and the diary, encourages in moments of discouragement, holds the person accountable.
- Recommended pace: a few minutes a day at first, then once a week once the habit is set.
- If there is no human coach yet, the diary serves as self-coaching, and this skill plays the coach's role via `/harada-coach diary`. But point out that a human reader changes the quality of the commitment: when you know you'll be read, you write better.
