# Mode D: team goal and developing people

The method works for one person, a team or an entire organization. A team is made of individuals: when each one progresses, the whole progresses. The best teams are made of excellent individuals who collaborate and respect each other's mastery.

## Breaking down a collective goal into individual goals (the book's protocol)

1. **Announce the shared goal** to everyone (example: "reach {figure} by {date}").
2. Each person sets **their own goal** that contributes to the shared goal.
3. Each person writes their purposes from the **4 perspectives** (tangible for me, tangible for others, intangible for me, intangible for others).
4. People **share** their goals and purposes with each other, then improve them.
5. Each person **presents** their goal to the group.
6. Each person writes **what they will concretely do** to reach the shared goal and their own.

Then, each person who wishes runs the full mode A. The forms can be posted on the wall so everyone sees the others' goals (a practice described in several companies in the book).

Key principle: instead of telling people what to do, share the organization's goal and ask them how they would reach it. A chosen goal motivates far more than an imposed quota.

## Link with business planning (Hoshin Kanri)

Hoshin Kanri (policy deployment) connects the long-term vision to daily activities, through a back-and-forth between hierarchical levels ("catchball"): no goal is adopted until the means to reach it are agreed. The Harada Method adds the personal dimension: when the process includes each person's personal goals, commitment increases. Start simple, from the existing system, and train managers in coaching above all.

## Mentoring a newcomer

Model described in the book:
- On arrival, the new employee sets demanding development goals with the organization for a given period (often one year).
- A **mentor** is assigned to them for the period. They interact almost every day.
- Central tool: the **daily diary**, with an extra page for observations, instructions, reminders, problems and questions for the mentor. The mentor writes advice and encouragement directly in the diary (in red ink in the book) and discusses it face to face.
- The diary teaches preparation (organizing your day and prioritizing) and reflection (what happened, what you learned).

## The manager as coach

- One of a manager's primary roles is to help each person develop to the fullest. They act as a coach.
- Developing people does not have to be expensive: when someone chooses what they want to improve, they look for their own training.
- Evening questions for small teams, recommended by the book: "What can we do to avoid repeating today's problems?", "What can we do to serve customers better?", "What can we do to be more self-reliant?"
- A 5 to 10 minute ritual before opening or production: in the morning to motivate, in the evening to congratulate and share the day's problems and their solutions.
- Skills matrix: list the skills needed and, for each person, the ones they master; certify and have the most skilled people train others.

## Differences between a leader and an old-style manager (summary from the book)

The leader is on the ground, takes care of people, loves their work, uses checklists, drives change, defends their teams, delegates and trusts, keeps an open door, encourages everyone to identify and solve problems and to propose small improvements, helps each person establish an annual development plan, leads by example, develops people who may surpass them, respects everyone. The old-style manager stays in meetings, keeps their distance, resists change, holds on to power, is distrustful, lets problems hide for fear of sanctions and has no development plan for their teams.
