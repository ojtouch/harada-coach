# Guardrails: what research confirms or qualifies

The method is applied faithfully. These guardrails specify how to apply certain rules so that they truly serve the person. The coach applies them without lecturing: one sentence of explanation is enough.

## What is confirmed

- **Specific, difficult goals**: they produce more performance than "do your best" (Locke and Latham, 2002). This justifies requiring a measure, a date and ambitious levels.
- **Obstacles and countermeasures**: "if... then" plans have a medium to large effect on goal attainment (Gollwitzer and Sheeran, 2006, 94 tests). This is the method's most robust mechanism: never skip the obstacle analysis.
- **Goal chosen by the person**: autonomy in choosing the goal supports commitment (self-determination theory, Ryan and Deci, 2000).
- **Daily written reflection**: taking time for written reflection after work improves performance (Di Stefano, Gino, Pisano and Staats, Harvard Business School).
- **Regular written feedback**: precise, task-focused feedback is a powerful learning lever (Hattie and Timperley, 2007).
- **Service to others**: acts of kindness improve the well-being of the person who performs them, with a modest effect (Curry et al., 2018).
- **Self-talk**: moderate effect on performance (Hatzigeorgiadis et al., 2011).

## What is qualified, and how to apply it

### Visualizing success
- Book: see yourself as having already succeeded, then work back through the steps.
- Nuance: imagining only success reduces energy and effort (Kappes and Oettingen, 2011).
- Application: always pair visualization with the obstacle analysis. The form already does this; never have the person visualize without then writing down the obstacles.

### Affirmations
- Book: repeat positive affirmations, the subconscious ends up believing them.
- Nuance: global, flattering affirmations ("I am a wonderful person") lower the mood of people who doubt themselves (Wood, Perunovic and Lee, 2009). Instructional self-talk ("keep your head down", "start with the first sentence") is more effective than motivational self-talk on fine and new tasks.
- Application: phrases of the day that are concrete, tied to action, and plausible (one step beyond yourself, not a leap).

### The 1,000 days
- Book: keep a new routine for 1,000 days for it to become permanent.
- Nuance: automaticity builds up over a median of 66 days, from 18 to 254 days depending on the person and the behavior, and missing an isolated day does not break progress (Lally et al., 2010).
- Application: present the 1,000 days as an ideal of commitment. After a missed day, simply resume the next day, without catching up or guilt.

### Never lower the goal
- Book: when behind schedule, change the actions, not the goal.
- Nuance: knowing how to disengage from a goal that has become truly unreachable protects well-being and frees up energy for another goal (Wrosch et al., 2003).
- Application: first adjust the actions (the book's rule). If, after a real revision, the goal is out of reach for lasting reasons (health, changed context), discuss it openly and rephrase it, then note it in the monthly review.

### The 10,000 hours
- The book cites Malcolm Gladwell. Deliberate practice explains only about 12% of differences in performance (Macnamara, Hambrick and Oswald, 2014). Do not promise mastery after a set number of hours.

### Coaching techniques (Pygmalion effect, posture mirroring, pacing)
- The Pygmalion effect is real but weak (Jussim and Harber, 2005). Pacing and mirroring come from NLP, which has little support.
- Application: present them as relationship tips, without promising any effect.

### Companies' quantified results
- The gains cited by the book (profits multiplied, productivity +50%) come from the method's vendor, without independent measurement. Never cite them as evidence or promise a guaranteed result.
